# The Six Dimensions Framework

The AI Diffusion Cube framework exists to compress the time, cost, and risk for anyone attempting to deploy AI at scale in public-interest contexts. It structures the lived experience of deployers — government officials, cooperative leaders, civil society implementers — into six orthogonal dimensions that together cover every decision domain where deployments succeed or fail. The framework is a capture lens: when ingesting a new deployment or answering an adopter question, these six dimensions are the categories through which evidence is organised and retrieved.

## Why these six dimensions

Field evidence from agricultural, health, livelihoods, and education deployments consistently shows that technology — the model, the infrastructure, the architecture — accounts for roughly 30% of what determines whether a deployment succeeds. The remaining 70% is institutional, human, and operational: who authorised the deployment, how partners were aligned, whether frontline workers absorbed the change, and whether a governance structure outlasts the pilot.

This 30/70 split has practical consequences for how a next adopter should allocate attention. A technically sound system can fail because no named government official holds accountability for it. A well-governed initiative can stall because the workforce training model did not account for low-literacy contexts. The six dimensions are the minimum set needed to cover both the technology 30% and the non-technology 70% without gaps.

The dimensions are orthogonal by design. Each answers a different question that an adopter must resolve. Overlapping them produces redundancy without additional insight; collapsing them produces dangerous gaps. A deployment that documents architecture thoroughly but ignores ecosystem design will miss the coordination problems that stall most deployments before they reach scale.

## The six dimensions

### A — Problem Orientation
*Question it answers: Where do I start?*
Problem Orientation establishes the foundation before any technology choice is made. It forces precision on who is being served, what problem they have that existing systems cannot solve, what constraints govern their access (language, literacy, connectivity), and what success looks like from their perspective. Without this clarity, deployments solve the wrong problem or build the wrong interface and discover the error only after scale.

### B — Architecture
*Question it answers: What do I build with?*
Architecture covers the technology stack — AI models, compute, data infrastructure, application channels — and the design decisions that keep the system flexible and evolvable. This includes how data is held and accessed (federated versus centralised), how vendor lock-in is avoided, what safety and moderation systems are in place, and how the AI connects to legacy institutional systems and existing workflows.

### C — Institution
*Question it answers: What kind of undertaking is this?*
Institution covers the deploying organisation — its structures, authority, capabilities, and funding mechanisms. The key insight from field evidence is that institutional decisions (who sponsors the deployment, who is accountable when something goes wrong, how procurement is navigated) determine whether the technology ever reaches users. Most deployments that fail do so in this dimension, not in the technology.

### D — Ecosystem
*Question it answers: Who does what, and how do we work together?*
Ecosystem covers the partners, enablers, and stakeholders assembled to execute the deployment. No single organisation deploys AI at public scale alone. This dimension covers who plays what role, who holds the network together when partners have competing priorities, how trust is established and maintained across organisational boundaries, and what coordination mechanisms sustain alignment over time.

### E — Workforce
*Question it answers: How do my people absorb this — and stay capable?*
Workforce covers the people who carry the deployment into daily practice — field workers, extension officers, call centre staff, teachers. It addresses when and how they are trained, whether there is resistance and what addresses it, how training reaches low-literacy or low-connectivity contexts, and whether staff retain capability when the system is unavailable. Technology that the workforce cannot absorb does not scale.

### F — Operating Model
*Question it answers: What makes this last beyond the pilot?*
Operating Model covers sustainability: what the deployment costs to build and run, what is measured to know it is working, who owns operations after the pilot ends, how governance is structured, and what course-correction mechanisms exist when the field reveals problems the design did not anticipate. Pilots that lack a credible operating model do not become deployments.
