**Who owns the problem and solution**
The framing is that Blue Dots is shared public infrastructure — comparable to UPI. No single actor "owns" it in the sense of controlling it. But governance authority sits clearly with the **district administration**. They govern the data for their district — accountable for keeping the rails open, the ecosystem live, and participants in control of their own data. Critically, this is described as *governance, not ownership*. The participants own their own Blue Dots — they can update, pause, or delete their data. That's built into the architecture, not a policy promise.

**Who owns the infrastructure**
The shared rails are operated by a **state government department** — the skilling mission, MSME department, or labour department, depending on the primary use case. So the operating layer sits at state level, the governance layer sits at district level, and the data itself sits with participants. The district administration and state operator function as *data fiduciaries* — accountable for lawful processing, not owners of the data.

**State Sponsor — more than funding**
The State Sponsor (a Mission Director or Secretary) does provide budget sanction, but the documented role is broader: they provide **political cover**, particularly for interdepartmental coordination. Blue Dots typically needs the skilling mission, MSME department, and labour department to cooperate — and that cross-departmental alignment is exactly where a district-level actor lacks the authority to push. The State Sponsor supplies that. It's described as a mandatory element of the ecosystem, not an optional one.

**District Champion — the spine**
This is the District Collector or Chief Development Officer. Their role is convening and signalling seriousness: they bring the first aggregators to the table and make clear the deployment matters institutionally. The pathway is blunt about why this role is non-negotiable — *without a committed District Champion, the ecosystem has no spine and the map goes dormant*. In Ghaziabad, the CDO played this role. The District Champion is the one who makes local actors believe this is real enough to invest their own time in.

### Assistant

Let me lay out what the pathway documents on architecture — and then flag where the line between shared infrastructure and end-user apps gets blurry in the documentation.

**The shared infrastructure — four DPGs**

Blue Dots runs on four open-source building blocks:

1. **AI Diffusion DPGs** — the foundational voice AI layer. This is the same seven open-source building blocks as MahaVistaar (Knowledge Engine, Memory Layer, Trust Layer, Agent Core, Action Gateway, Reach Layer, Learning Layer). It's unbundled and works with any speech models and LLMs. This is what handles the 2–3 minute voice call in any language and turns spoken intent into structured data.

2. **Signal DPG** — manages each Blue Dot signal. It structures a signal as a location-anchored Blue Dot, stores it under consent-governed rules, handles matching and connection flows, and feeds live signals out to consuming platforms.

3. **Aggregator DPG** — gives each aggregator (an ITI, MSME association, NGO) a platform to register, onboard participants at scale, track Blue Dot status (active, at-risk, satisfied, stalled), and issue digitally verifiable credentials.

4. **Facilitator DPG** — gives the district administration tools to monitor the supply-demand gap, onboard aggregators and innovators, and keep the ecosystem healthy.

So the shared rails are: **voice intake → signal management/matching/storage → aggregator onboarding tooling → district monitoring tooling.** Data lives on these shared public rails, operated by the state department, governed by the district, owned by participants.

**What's an end-user app vs the shared layer**

Here's where it's clearer in principle than in documented detail. The architecture is explicitly designed so that **innovators plug into the shared map** — staffing firms, assessment providers, skilling organisations, transport and financial services providers, startups. Named ones in the pilots: Head Held High, Recex, JobsUp, TRRAIN, Proof of Skill, Digital Labour Chowk. These are the end-user-facing applications. They consume the live signals from the Signal DPG and build their own services on top.

So the division is roughly:
- **Shared layer (the rails):** voice intake, Blue Dot creation, storage, matching, status tracking, district monitoring — the four DPGs
- **App layer (built by innovators):** the actual services participants and businesses interact with for placement, assessment, skilling, transport, finance

And the voice call itself is interesting — it's both. A participant *becomes* a Blue Dot through the shared voice layer directly (that's not an innovator app), but then innovators build richer services on the resulting signals.

**Where the documentation thins out — gaps for you**

- **The exact API/contract between the Signal DPG and the consuming innovator apps** isn't documented. How does an innovator actually plug in? What does the integration look like? Not specified.
- **What signals the innovators feed back** is described directionally — "as innovators plug in, their activity adds new signal types (application counts, shortlisting patterns, placement outcomes)" — but the mechanism for that feedback loop into the shared layer isn't detailed.
- **Where the boundary sits for matching** — does the Signal DPG do the matching, or do innovator apps do their own matching on top of raw signals? The pathway says the Signal DPG "handles matching and connection flows," but then innovators also clearly do matching (staffing firms). The division of labour between platform matching and app-level matching isn't crisp.
- **Open protocol layer** — the agriculture deployments use Beckn for open-network interoperability. Whether Blue Dots uses the same protocol layer for the innovator integration isn't documented.

That last cluster — the **integration contract between shared rails and innovator apps, and where matching actually happens** — is probably the most important architectural gap to fill, because it's the seam that determines whether the "shared infrastructure, many apps" model actually works as designed or just on paper. Worth putting directly to EkStep, who built the DPGs.
