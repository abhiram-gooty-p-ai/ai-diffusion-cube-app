### Assistant

Helpful — so the breakdown sits at two connected points: people don't know **what's available to them specifically**, and even when they do, they don't know **how to actually get it**. That's a discovery-and-navigation problem, and it's worth holding both halves together because a solution that does one without the other tends to disappoint people.

Let me reflect back one thing I'm noticing, and then ask you the next question.

The phrase "available to **them**" is doing a lot of work. A general list of all minority schemes is easy to publish — and largely already exists on portals. The hard, valuable part is the *to them* — matching a specific person's situation (their community, income, age, state, what they're trying to do) to the handful of schemes that actually apply, and then walking them to the door. That personalisation is usually where these efforts either succeed or become another ignored website.

So before we go anywhere near what to build, I want to understand the people you're trying to reach, because it shapes everything downstream.

**What do you know about how these citizens would actually reach a service like this?** Specifically:

- Do most of them have smartphones, or are many on basic phones?
- What languages do they speak, and is literacy a constraint for a meaningful share of them?
- Are they more likely to come to something themselves, or do they need to be reached through someone they already trust — a local office, a community organisation, a frontline worker?

You may not have precise answers, and rough ones are fine. I ask because there is a strong, repeated lesson in the deployments I can draw on: the access constraints of the hardest-to-reach person are not a detail to handle later — they decide the core design. A voice call in the local language on any phone reaches a very different population than an app does.
