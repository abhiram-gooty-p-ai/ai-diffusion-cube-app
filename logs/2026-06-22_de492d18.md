The reason I ask: a few of the deployments I can draw on have worked through exactly the hard parts of multilingual, multi-department citizen interaction, but the relevant lessons differ depending on those two answers.

A couple of things worth flagging early, drawn from what's been learned elsewhere. First, "all departments" is the part that tends to trip people up — not the language, not the chatbot. When MahaVistaar in Maharashtra connected agriculture advisory across several institutions, the genuinely hard work was inter-departmental data sharing and getting one institution to say "this runs in my name" and own accountability for what the system tells citizens. Grievances across all departments multiplies that challenge considerably. Worth thinking about whether you have one institution with authority to align the others.

Second, on the multilingual piece — there's solid, reusable experience here. MahaVistaar and Amul both went voice-first specifically because text-based apps exclude the people who most need to lodge a grievance. If your citizens include people without smartphones or text literacy, voice over a basic phone call may matter more than the chatbot framing suggests.

Tell me more about who you're serving and what "handling a grievance" actually means in your context, and I can get more specific.

And two things I'd want to understand — when a citizen lodges a grievance, does the AI need to understand their intent well enough to route it to the correct department automatically, or does your existing system already handle routing once the grievance is captured? A couple of patterns from elsewhere that map closely onto what you're describing:

On capturing intent from speech — Blue Dots AI does almost exactly this on the input side. A citizen speaks for two to three minutes on any phone in any language, and the AI understands their intent and structures it into a record. That's the same problem as a citizen describing a grievance in their own words and the system turning it into a structured, routable entry. The harder-than-expected part there was the AI understanding vernacular, hyperlocal language well enough to structure messy real-world speech — not standard textbook language. A domain glossary bridging how citizens actually describe problems to your departmental categories tends to be necessary; general translation models stumble on the specialised terms.

On status updates and reaching out — the deployments that became genuinely valued were the ones that didn't just wait for citizens to call in, but proactively pushed updates ("your grievance has moved to this stage"). Worth considering whether your AI layer does outbound too, not just inbound.

One thing I'd gently surface: your existing system speaks on behalf of all those departments. When the AI answers a citizen about their grievance, whose voice is it speaking in, and who's accountable if it gives a wrong status or misroutes something? In every deployment I can draw on, the AI never spoke as itself — it spoke as a named institution the citizen already trusts, and that institution owned accountability for the answers. With grievances spanning many departments, worth being clear on whether the E-Governance Department holds that accountability or whether it sits with each receiving department.

What's the citizen access picture — smartphones, basic phones, or both?
