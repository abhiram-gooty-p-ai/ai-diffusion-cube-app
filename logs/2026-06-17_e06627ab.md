I'll be honest about what I can and can't give you. I can't hand you a finished initiative design — that would be me inventing structure I don't have evidence for. But I can tell you, from across six documented deployments, where states actually get stuck, and that's the more useful starting point.

The consistent finding is the 70/30 split: roughly 70% of what determines whether a deployment survives is non-technology — institution, ecosystem, workforce, operating model — and only 30% is the tech. The technology, once built by a pioneer, transfers. What doesn't transfer, and what kills deployments, is institutional: no one willing to say "this runs in my name," political cover that vanishes when a champion moves, or pilot funding with no path to sustained operations.

The single most decisive thing across every deployment was the network operator — the institution that holds the deployment in its name and can align the others. Ethiopia's three-month timeline was possible because ATI had cross-institutional authority before anything began. So for an initiative helping multiple states, the highest-leverage design question is: who plays the network operator role in each state, and does your initiative help identify and equip that person?
