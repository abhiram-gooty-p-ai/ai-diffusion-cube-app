That's a meaningful place to be — a neutral convening role across multiple states is actually one of the harder and more important positions in this work, because the thing you're designing isn't a single deployment, it's the conditions under which many deployments can happen.

Before going further into the design, the most useful thing I can offer is an anchor from what's already been built — because there's a live example that's close to what you're describing.

India's national agriculture layer, Bharat-VISTAAR, was built precisely to solve the multi-state problem. The core insight was this: without a shared national layer, every state has to build its own knowledge base, scheme connectivity, and data integration from scratch — duplicating the same infrastructure over and over. So the design became a national layer that states connect to as nodes, each keeping its own identity and local specificity, while drawing on shared national assets (the scheme data, the research advisory corpus, the farmer registry integration). Maharashtra doesn't carry the full cost of the national knowledge base; that sits at the national level and is amortised across every state that joins.

What makes this relevant to you: the states that connected didn't all build the same way. Maharashtra built the pioneer deployment from scratch. Bihar built independently with its own partners and then connected retroactively. Both work — but they cost different amounts of time and money to get there.

So here's the one thing I'd want to understand before we go further into your design: when you say "help multiple states adopt" — are you imagining building shared infrastructure that states plug into, or are you imagining helping each state run its own adoption process well? Those are two quite different initiatives, and the answer shapes almost everything else.

It happened because the pioneer deployment codified its architecture, governance frameworks, failure modes, and deployment playbooks into reusable assets — and each subsequent deployment started from the highest point the prior one had reached rather than rebuilding.

Here's the pattern that matters most for you. Across every documented deployment, the failure surface splits roughly **70% non-technology, 30% technology**. States that don't know how to do this well almost always misallocate against that split — they spend their planning energy on model selection, data pipelines, and architecture (the 30% that is real but recoverable and, importantly, *already largely solved* in the reusable components), and under-invest in the institutional, ecosystem, and workforce work (the 70% where deployments actually die).

So "doing this well" is mostly about helping states get good at the parts they're least inclined to focus on. Concretely, the documented evidence points to a few things states consistently underthink:

- **Framing.** A state that frames this as a technology project gets a pilot, a report, and nothing after. A state that frames it as a transformation initiative — embedded in something like an agriculture roadmap (Bihar's 4th Agriculture Roadmap, Ethiopia's Digital Agriculture Roadmap) — gets multi-year funding and survives staff transfers. This costs nothing but clarity, and it's the single highest-leverage thing you could standardise.

- **Workforce.** Bihar trained 15,000+ extension workers across 38 districts *as part of the rollout*, not bolted on at the end. Most states treat training as a launch-day afterthought. This is a design decision that has to be made early.

- **The pilot-to-sustainability transition.** States routinely build a pilot with project funding and excited volunteers, and have no plan for who owns steady-state operations when that ends.

Here's where I'd want to focus next, because it determines the *form* your knowledge transfer should take. "Knowing how to do this well" can be delivered in very different ways — a playbook states read themselves, a hands-on team that embeds with each state, or a structured peer connection to states that have already done it. Do you have a sense yet of how hands-on your organisation can be with each state — or is that still open?
