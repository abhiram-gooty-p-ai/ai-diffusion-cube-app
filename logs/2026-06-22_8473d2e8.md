That's a fair place to start — "AI for farmers" gets said a lot without much meaning behind it. Let me make it concrete, because there's quite a bit of real deployment experience here.

The recurring problem across agriculture is that extension systems can't reach everyone. In several states the ratio has been something like one agriculture officer for every 2,000 farmers — so most farmers never get timely, personalised advice on their crops, weather, market prices, or government schemes. The information exists, but it's locked up in different departments that were never designed to talk to each other. A farmer asking "my paddy is standing and rain is coming — should I harvest now?" needs weather, market prices, and crop-stage advice answered together, in their own language, in seconds. That's the gap AI has been used to close.

A few things others have actually built that might resonate:
- A farmer calls a number on any phone — even a basic feature phone — asks a question in their own language by voice, and gets an answer drawing on agricultural research, weather, and scheme data. This is what Maharashtra did, reaching farmers at a scale human officers never could.
- A cooperative surfacing its own data back to members — Amul took 50 years of dairy records and made it so a farmer could ask about her own animal's history and health over a phone call.

That's a well-defined problem, and there's strong experience on exactly this.

The closest match is Bihar Krishi — they faced the same thing and unified 50+ government schemes onto a single farmer-facing platform. The result: 38,000+ scheme applications submitted through it. The core insight from their work is that low utilization usually isn't a scheme design problem — it's a discovery and navigation problem. Farmers don't know which schemes they're eligible for, can't navigate the application process, and have no accessible guide. With 20 separate schemes, the farmer has no way of holding all of that in their head or knowing where to even start.

What AI changed: a farmer can call and ask in plain language — "I have two acres, I grow paddy, what am I entitled to?" — and the system figures out eligibility across all schemes and walks them through applying. MahaVistaar does this across 40+ schemes, accessible by voice on any phone. The scheme status and eligibility data stays where it lives in each department's systems; the AI connects to it at the moment of the question rather than copying it all into one place. That matters for your departments retaining control of their own data.

A couple of things to think through for your situation:

First — where does your scheme eligibility data actually live? Is it in MahaDBT-style portals, separate departmental systems, or partly offline? The architecture connects to live systems, so knowing what's queryable shapes what's possible.

Second — the application itself. Surfacing eligibility is one thing; the farmer then has to actually apply. Bihar paired this with extension workers as the last-mile help for cases the system couldn't fully complete. Do you have a field workforce you'd want in that role?

What does the current scheme application journey look like for a Jharkhand farmer today — where exactly do they fall off?

This is exactly the pattern MahaVistaar and Bihar started from. The Maharashtra framing was sharp: a farmer would need a smartphone, reliable internet, enough literacy, and the right language just to access what the government portal already held. That excludes most smallholders — so the portal holds the information but the farmer can't get to it. The fix was voice-first: the farmer calls a number on any phone, asks in their own language, and the AI navigates the portal's information on their behalf. No smartphone, no literacy, no form-filling required. The information you've already put on the portal becomes the knowledge base the AI draws from.

On the field workers not working — it's worth being precise about *why* before assuming AI replaces them. A few common reasons others have hit: too few of them relative to farmers, no easy way for them to navigate 20 schemes themselves, or no clear handoff between "farmer has a question" and "application gets completed." Bihar's approach was telling — they didn't drop field workers, they trained 15,000+ of them and shifted their role. The AI handled the high-volume routine eligibility questions; the field worker focused on actually getting applications across the line for cases that needed a human. The worker stops being the scarce first point of contact and becomes the closer.

So two questions that would sharpen this:

When you say the field workers aren't working — is it that there are too few, that they don't have the tools to navigate the schemes, or that farmers just aren't reaching them in the first place?

And for a farmer to actually apply to a scheme on your portal today — can that be done over a phone conversation, or does it ultimately require a smartphone, a login, document uploads? That determines whether the AI can carry the farmer all the way through, or only up to the point of "here's what you're eligible for, now a field worker helps you apply."
