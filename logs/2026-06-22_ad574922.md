Two opposite failure modes show up: clinicians rubber-stamping AI outputs without review, or reflexively overriding them. Both eliminate the human judgment the design depends on. ### Assistant

That's the question I'd pause hardest on — and I'd push back before you commit to it.

Here's the pattern from the deployments I've seen. None of them put the AI's output directly into a high-stakes decision without a human in the loop. In agriculture, a wrong crop advisory costs a season — bad, recoverable. The deployers were still careful: MahaVistaar and Amul both run an *independent moderation layer* (a separate model reviewing outputs before they reach the user) precisely because confident-but-wrong answers reaching someone who acts on them is the failure mode that does real damage.

A diagnostic tool going straight to patients is a much sharper version of that. Your 95% means 1 in 20 outputs is wrong — and a patient has no clinical judgment to catch the wrong one. They can't tell a good answer from a dangerous one. With a farmer asking "should I harvest now," the downside is a lost crop. With a patient acting on a wrong diagnosis, the downside is harm to a person.

The other thing the agriculture deployments make clear: the AI worked *because* it borrowed trust from an institution the user already trusted, and because a credible human was somewhere in the chain when judgment was needed. Amul's veterinary AI handles first-line queries, then escalates to the vet network for anything complex. The AI didn't replace the expert on hard cases — it freed the expert for them.

So the design question I'd put to you: **could this tool sit behind a clinician or health worker rather than in front of the patient directly?** A health worker who uses it as a reference layer — and is trained on when to trust it and when to verify — preserves the judgment that catches the dangerous 1-in-20. That's the model that's worked in the deployments I have evidence from.

I want to be clear this is cross-domain reasoning — I'm transferring a pattern from agriculture and a dairy cooperative, not citing a telemedicine deployment. I don't have lived health experience to show you. But the structural risk — high-stakes output, no human judgment in the path, a user who can't evaluate the answer — is one these deployments deliberately designed around.

What's making you lean toward patient-direct rather than clinician-mediated? Is it a reach problem — not enough clinicians to go around?
