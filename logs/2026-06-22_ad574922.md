Before I dig in — let me be honest about what I can and can't offer here. The deployments I have documented experience from are all in agriculture and livelihoods (in India and Ethiopia). I don't have a telemedicine or health diagnostic deployment to point you to directly.

That said, a lot of what determines whether a deployment like yours works — or quietly fails — has very little to do with the 95% accuracy figure. So let me start by reframing your question, because the thing the vendor is selling you (model accuracy) sits in roughly 30% of what will actually make or break this. The other 70% is institutional, ecosystem, workforce, and operating-model work that no accuracy number addresses.

A few things I'd worry about, drawn from patterns that have held across every deployment I've seen:

**Whose voice does the tool speak in, and do patients and clinicians trust it?** In every documented deployment, the AI never spoke as itself — it borrowed trust from an institution the user already trusted (a state agriculture department, a 50-year-old cooperative). For a diagnostic tool, this is sharper: if a wrong output reaches a patient and they act on it, who is accountable, and is that clear from the start? "95% accurate" means 1 in 20 outputs is wrong — in a diagnostic context, what happens to that one matters enormously, and you'll want a moderation/verification layer that catches harmful outputs *before* they reach the patient, not a complaints process after.

**What does the clinician do with the output?** The recurring question is whether your frontline people stay more capable or become dependent. Two opposite failure modes show up: clinicians rubber-stamping AI outputs without review, or reflexively overriding them. Both eliminate the human judgment the design depends on. How are you training for *when to verify* — not just how to use the tool?
