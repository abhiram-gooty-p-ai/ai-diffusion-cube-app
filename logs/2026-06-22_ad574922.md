A diagnostic tool going straight to patients is a much sharper version of that. Your 95% means 1 in 20 outputs is wrong — and a patient has no clinical judgment to catch the wrong one. They can't tell a good answer from a dangerous one. With a farmer asking "should I harvest now," the downside is a lost crop. With a patient acting on a wrong diagnosis, the downside is harm to a person.

The other thing the agriculture deployments make clear: the AI worked *because* it borrowed trust from an institution the user already trusted, and because a credible human was somewhere in the chain when judgment was needed. Amul's veterinary AI handles first-line queries, then escalates to the vet network for anything complex. The AI didn't replace the expert on hard cases — it freed the expert for them.

So the design question I'd put to you: **could this tool sit behind a clinician or health worker rather than in front of the patient directly?** A health worker who uses it as a reference layer — and is trained on when to trust it and when to verify — preserves the judgment that catches the dangerous 1-in-20. That's exactly the constraint the agriculture deployments started from — and it's worth seeing how they handled it, because they faced the *same* math you're facing.

Bihar had roughly one agriculture field officer for every 2,000 farmers. Ethiopia's extension ratio was comparable. The whole reason they reached for AI was that the human workforce could not scale to the population. So your instinct — "there aren't enough health workers, so let the AI reach patients directly" — is the right diagnosis of the problem. It's the *solution* shape I'd question.

Here's what they did instead of removing the human: they used AI to **multiply the reach of the scarce human**, not bypass them. Two patterns are worth your attention:

**The first-line / escalation split.** Amul has 1,400 vets for 3.6 million farmers and 22 million cattle — an impossible ratio, exactly like yours. The AI handles the routine first-line queries that would otherwise consume the vet's time or go unanswered, and escalates anything complex to the vet network. The scarce expert's time gets concentrated on the cases that actually need judgment. The vet's reach effectively multiplies, but the judgment stays in the chain for hard cases. For you, the equivalent question is: which queries are genuinely low-risk and self-resolving, and which must route to a health worker? That triage boundary is your most important design decision — and it's a clinical decision, not a technical one.

**Use as training, capability over dependency.** In agriculture, farmers weren't trained before access — they called a number and asked. That worked *because the downside of a wrong answer was bounded*. For health, you don't have that luxury, which is why the escalation path matters even more.

So the reframe I'd offer: the workforce shortage doesn't argue for patient-direct — it argues for an AI that lets each health worker cover far more patients by absorbing the routine load and surfacing the cases that need them. That's how these deployments turned an impossible staffing ratio into a working system without removing the human from high-stakes decisions.

I should be honest again that this is transferred reasoning, not a documented health deployment — but the underlying problem (scarce frontline workers, large population, need for trusted advisory) is structurally identical to what these deployments solved.

Two things I'd want you to work through next: what's your triage boundary — which queries are safe to auto-resolve and which must escalate?
