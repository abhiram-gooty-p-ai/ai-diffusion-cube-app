### Assistant

For packaging lived experience so a new adopter can find their own way, a pure step-by-step sequence tends to fail — it works only if the next adopter's context matches the original exactly, which it never does. A new adopter in a different state, with a different language, different connectivity, and a different downstream use case can't just follow Nandurbar's steps in order.

What transfers across contexts is the *decisions and the failure modes* — and what doesn't transfer is the specific sequence, the specific partners, the specific corpus. This is actually a documented pattern: the architecture and governance know-how travel; the ecosystem, the language corpus, and the institutional context have to be rebuilt each time. So a decision framework anchored in lived evidence is the more honest packaging.

But the most useful shape is a hybrid: organise it as a **decision framework**, but at each decision point, ground it in *what Astitva actually did, what they discovered, and what they'd warn you about*. So the adopter isn't just told "decide your inbound/outbound scope" — they're told why, with the evidence that MahaVISTAAR had to rearchitect six months in because it didn't. The decision is the transferable unit; the story is what makes the decision real.

One caution worth flagging as you build this. If you're packaging Astitva specifically, the lived substance is uneven across the journey. The front end is rich — the data collection model, the quality pipeline, the read-vs-conversational and 8kHz audio lessons are all concrete and field-tested. But the back end thins out considerably: who owns the Dehvali Bhili capability in MahaVISTAAR today when it degrades, how the feedback loop from field to model actually works, what it cost, and what the error-to-fix path looks like — these are not documented. A playbook structured as confident step-by-step would paper over those gaps. A decision framework lets you be honest: "here is the decision you face; here is where even the pioneer hasn't figured it out yet."

Do you want to work through what the decision points should be across the whole journey — including the thin back-end ones, which are arguably the most valuable to flag precisely because no one has solved them?

A model trained only on people reading written sentences aloud performs badly in real, natural conversation, because the rhythm and vocabulary differ. And audio recorded on a clean smartphone (16kHz) sounds different from what comes through a phone line (8kHz) — a model trained only on clean audio degrades on actual calls. 
Astitva: Both were learned the hard way. Initial collection was read-speech only, and the model failed in conversation — so a separate spontaneous-speech track had to be added. And data collected at 16kHz underperformed on live 8kHz telephony until lower-quality audio was added to the training set. Both are now treated as non-negotiable.

Astitva: Used a component architecture (ASR + LLM + TTS) precisely because they needed domain control and guardrails. **A few coverage gaps worth a deliberate decision:**
