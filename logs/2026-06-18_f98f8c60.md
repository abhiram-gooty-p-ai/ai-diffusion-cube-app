### User

I am trying to design a national initiative for states to use AI in agriculture. Tell me what are the key aspects that I should cover in the design

---

### Assistant

This is a strong place to be starting — a national-layer-for-states design is exactly the shape that Bharat-VISTAAR took in India and what Ethiopia's ATI built in a different institutional context, so there's relevant lived experience here.

Before I lay out aspects, one thing I want to understand, because it changes what matters most in the design: **when you say "for states to use," what's the relationship you're imagining between the national layer and the states?**

Specifically — are you picturing the national initiative *building* the AI systems that states then run, or building a common layer (shared data, shared advisory content, shared building blocks) that states connect their *own* systems into, each keeping their own identity? These two lead to quite different designs, and the deployments I know took the second path deliberately.

I ask because the single most load-bearing finding across these deployments is counterintuitive for a national initiative: **the technology is roughly 30% of the challenge. The other 70% is institutional, ecosystem, and workforce.** A national design that spends most of its attention on the platform and the models is allocating effort against the smaller part of the problem.

So rather than dump a checklist, let me name the aspects that the lived experience says matter most for a *national* design specifically, and you tell me where you want to go deeper:

1. **What each state actually owns versus what the national layer carries.** In Bharat-VISTAAR, the national layer held the things no single state should rebuild — the national knowledge base, scheme data, farmer registry integration — so each state didn't bear that cost. Getting this division right is the core architectural decision, and it's more an institutional question than a technical one.

2. **Who is accountable — the "this runs in my name" question.** Every deployment that worked had one institution willing to be accountable for outputs. At national level this gets harder because multiple ministries and agencies hold pieces. Ethiopia's answer was instructive: a cross-government transformation institute, not a single ministry, because no single ministry could align the others.

3. **How states come on board — and the two paths into it.** Some states will want to build on your shared building blocks from day one. Others will have already built something and want to connect later (Bihar did exactly this). A national design needs to accommodate both, or you exclude half your states.

4. **Trust — whose voice the AI speaks in.** This matters more than the technology. Farmers act on advice from an institution they already trust. At national level you have to decide whether the AI speaks as the national ministry, the state department, or both — and the answer shapes adoption.

5. **The frontline workforce.** Extension officers are the people who absorb this, and they're consistently underthought at the design stage. Bihar trained 15,000+ extension workers as part of the rollout, not after it.

6. **What makes it last past the launch.** Funding model, who runs steady-state operations, and the path from pilot to permanent — these are design decisions, not afterthoughts.

Which of these is most live for you right now? And the build-vs-connect question above — where's your thinking? That'll let me pull the specific evidence rather than keep talking in general terms.

### Assistant

That's a clear and important distinction — thank you. It changes the design quite a bit. You're not building infrastructure; you're building an **enabling programme**: design support plus funding for states to run their own pilots. That's closer to a grant-and-capability programme than a platform.

Let me be honest about something first: the deployments I have lived experience from were mostly the *states* and *cooperatives* doing the building — MahaVistaar, Bihar, Amul. I have rich knowledge of what those deployers struggled with. What I have *less* direct evidence on is the layer you're sitting at — the national body funding and shaping state pilots. So I'll be clear as I go about where I'm drawing on solid evidence versus where I'm reasoning from the deployment patterns.

With that caveat, here's where the lived experience speaks most directly to a design-and-fund initiative:

**1. What you require states to have answered before they get funded.** This is your highest-leverage design choice. The deployments that struggled didn't struggle on technology — they struggled because the problem was framed too broadly, no institution had agreed to be accountable, or there was no plan for life after the pilot. If your funding criteria force states to answer those questions *up front*, you prevent the most common failures by design. A few that the evidence says matter most as gating questions:
   - Can the state name the specific farmer and the specific problem — not "improve agriculture" but something a farmer would recognise?
   - Which state institution will say "this runs in my name" and be accountable for what the AI tells farmers?
   - What happens after your pilot money ends — who owns and funds steady-state?

**2. Designing against the pilot-forever trap.** This is the one I'd flag hardest for a fund. Pilots have grant money, energy, and weekly attention. Deployments have none of those. The single most common way these initiatives die is a successful pilot with no path to permanent. If your funding structures a pilot as an end in itself, you may be *funding* the failure mode. The states that lasted (Bihar, via its 4th Agriculture Roadmap) had embedded the work in a multi-year state commitment *before* the pilot — so the design question is whether you make that a condition of funding.

**3. Helping states reuse instead of rebuild.** The most striking pattern in this knowledge is deployment time compressing from nine months to three months to three weeks — because later deployers reused open building blocks and codified know-how instead of starting from scratch. For a national initiative, this is a real lever: you can steer states toward reusable open-source components rather than each commissioning a custom build. That alone could change the economics of every pilot you fund. (In India this was the OpenAgriNet set of components, supported by EkStep Foundation — if useful, that's a concrete starting point states could be pointed to.)

**4. The 70/30 in your design support.** Since the technology is the smaller part, your *design help* to states should weight accordingly. If your programme support is mostly about model selection and platform choice, you're helping states with 30% of their problem. The design support that's scarce and valuable is on the institutional, ecosystem, and workforce side — naming the accountable institution, assembling partners, training extension officers.

**5. A shared learning loop across states.** This is the one thing a national initiative can do that no individual state can. The compression above only happened because the pioneer's hard-won knowledge was captured and passed on. If your initiative captures what each funded state learns — what failed, what worked, what the real cost was — and feeds it to the next state, you make every subsequent pilot cheaper and faster. That's arguably the highest-value thing the national layer can contribute when it isn't building the technology itself.

Two honest gaps I want to name: I don't have a documented example of a *funding programme* designed this way, so points 1, 2, and 5 are me reasoning from what deployers struggled with, not from a fund that did it well. And I have almost no evidence on how you'd structure the funding mechanism itself — disbursement, milestones, evaluation criteria — that's outside what these deployments document.

Where do you want to go deeper? My instinct is that your funding criteria (point 1) and the pilot-to-permanent question (point 2) are where your design choices will do the most work — but tell me what's most live for you.
