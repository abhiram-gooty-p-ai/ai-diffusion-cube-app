Specifically — are you picturing the national initiative *building* the AI systems that states then run, or building a common layer (shared data, shared advisory content, shared building blocks) that states connect their *own* systems into, each keeping their own identity? These two lead to quite different designs, and the deployments I know took the second path deliberately.

I ask because the single most load-bearing finding across these deployments is counterintuitive for a national initiative: **the technology is roughly 30% of the challenge. The other 70% is institutional, ecosystem, and workforce.** A national design that spends most of its attention on the platform and the models is allocating effort against the smaller part of the problem.

So rather than dump a checklist, let me name the aspects that the lived experience says matter most for a *national* design specifically, and you tell me where you want to go deeper:

1. **What each state actually owns versus what the national layer carries.** In Bharat-VISTAAR, the national layer held the things no single state should rebuild — the national knowledge base, scheme data, farmer registry integration — so each state didn't bear that cost. Getting this division right is the core architectural decision, and it's more an institutional question than a technical one.

2. **Who is accountable — the "this runs in my name" question.** Every deployment that worked had one institution willing to be accountable for outputs. At national level this gets harder because multiple ministries and agencies hold pieces. Ethiopia's answer was instructive: a cross-government transformation institute, not a single ministry, because no single ministry could align the others.

3. **How states come on board — and the two paths into it.** Some states will want to build on your shared building blocks from day one. Others will have already built something and want to connect later (Bihar did exactly this). A national design needs to accommodate both, or you exclude half your states.

4. **Trust — whose voice the AI speaks in.** This matters more than the technology. Farmers act on advice from an institution they already trust. At national level you have to decide whether the AI speaks as the national ministry, the state department, or both — and the answer shapes adoption.

5. **The frontline workforce.** Extension officers are the people who absorb this, and they're consistently underthought at the design stage. Bihar trained 15,000+ extension workers as part of the rollout, not after it.

6. **What makes it last past the launch.** Funding model, who runs steady-state operations, and the path from pilot to permanent — these are design decisions, not afterthoughts.

**"What specific farmer and what specific problems?"**

Here the evidence is genuinely useful, because every deployment converged on the *same underlying problem*, framed from the farmer's side:

A smallholder farmer can't get timely, personalised advice because there's roughly one extension officer per 2,000 farmers (that was India's baseline; Ethiopia's was comparable). The information exists — it's just locked in institutions that don't talk to each other and isn't reachable by a farmer on a basic phone.

The specific problems that showed up as real, high-volume farmer demand were concrete: *"leaf curling on my chilli — what do I do?"*, *"my paddy is standing and rain is coming — harvest now or wait?"*, *"am I eligible for this scheme and what's the status of my application?"*, plus weather, pest alerts, and market prices. MahaVistaar's query logs showed crop/pest advisory was the dominant category (~205,000 queries/month).

**What this means for your design:** you don't need to specify the farmer for the states — but you *can* require each state to do exactly what these deployers did: name the farmer in their language and crop system, and define the problem as something a farmer would actually ask. A useful design device is to make states show *real farmer questions*, not a problem statement written by a department. That single requirement filters out technology-push proposals.

**"Which state institution should run it in its name?"**

The evidence is clear on the *type*, not the named body (which varies by country). In every case it was the institution that already holds credibility with farmers AND the authority to share data and stand behind advice:
- Maharashtra → the **State Department of Agriculture**
- Bihar → the **State Agriculture Department**, anchored in its multi-year Agriculture Roadmap
- Amul (cooperative) → the **cooperative itself**

The pattern: it's the agriculture department (or its equivalent) — because farmers trust it and it can authorise the data-sharing across universities, weather services, and scheme systems that makes the whole thing work. Ethiopia is the instructive exception: where no single ministry could align the others, a **cross-government transformation institute** held the accountable role.

**What this means for your design:** require each state to *name* the accountable institution as a condition of funding — and require evidence that it (a) has farmer credibility and (b) can authorise the necessary data-sharing. The instructive exception tells you to build in an alternative path for states where agriculture sits weakly across several agencies.

**"Who should own and fund steady-state?"**

This is where I have to be honest: the deployments are clearer on *what worked* than on a clean prescription, and none of them documents the funding mechanism in the detail you'd need.

What the evidence shows: steady-state ownership sat with **the same state institution that ran the pilot** — not the national body, not the technical partner. And survival past the pilot came from **embedding the work in a pre-existing multi-year state commitment** (Bihar's 4th Agriculture Roadmap; Ethiopia's seven-year Digital Agriculture Roadmap) *before* the pilot, so steady-state funding was already a state budget line, not a hope.

On *cost*, there's one usable anchor: a deployment of this kind ran roughly **$250,000 to set up and ~$250,000/year to sustain** — and the running cost dropped sharply when states moved from paid AI services to self-hosted open-source models (one deployment went from ~₹9 per query to ~₹0.05). So steady-state cost is real but not enormous, and it's controllable through architecture choices.

**What this means for your design:** the design choice the evidence supports is to **make state ownership of steady-state a funding condition** — specifically, evidence that the work is anchored in a state budget commitment or multi-year roadmap, not just a willingness letter. Your national money funds the pilot; it does *not* become the permanent funder. That's the design that avoids the pilot-forever trap.
